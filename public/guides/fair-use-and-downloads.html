Rights

Fair use, licensing, and the line between backup and republishing

The desk19 November 20267 min read

Most arguments about downloading go wrong at the first sentence, because two unrelated rulebooks get mixed together. One is copyright law: who owns a work and what you may do with a copy. The other is a platform's terms of service: a private contract that governs how you may use the site. A single download can satisfy one and break the other, and neither is a licence for the other.

Two rulebooks, not one

Copyright is public law. It grants the creator a set of exclusive rights — reproduction, distribution, public performance, derivative works — with exceptions that vary by country. Terms of service are private contract law: you agreed to them when you used the site, and breaking them is a matter between you and the site, generally enforced by blocking, suspension or account termination.

That distinction explains a familiar oddity: something can be legal to copy and still against a site's rules, or permitted by a site and still an infringement. The dowload button's absence is a policy choice, not a legal finding.

Permission from a platform is not permission from a creator. They are different signatures on different documents.

What the four factors really ask

In the United States, fair use is decided by weighing four factors. Other countries have narrower exceptions — fair dealing, private copying, quotation — so the analysis differs, but the shape of the question is similar.

  1. Purpose and character. Comment, criticism, teaching, news and parody sit more comfortably than commercial re-upload. A transformation that adds meaning helps; a straight mirror does not.
  2. Nature of the work. Factual and published material is treated more generously than unpublished, creative work.
  3. Amount taken. The whole thing is rarely the safe amount when a short excerpt would do the job.
  4. Effect on the market. If your use substitutes for a sale or a licence the owner offers, this factor usually decides the case.

Fair use is a defence you raise after being accused, not a permission slip you hold in advance. That is why "it's fair use" is a weak plan for anything you intend to publish at scale.

Where personal archiving usually sits

Keeping a copy of something you already have lawful access to, for your own later viewing, is treated differently across jurisdictions: explicitly permitted in some, tolerated in others, and technically an infringement in a few. What it is almost never is a public performance or a distribution — those require sharing the copy, which is the line described below.

Licences that make the question disappear

The cleanest downloads are the ones where the rights holder already said yes. Learn to read the label:

  • Public domain — no rights remain, or the creator waived them (CC0). Government and archival footage is often here, but always verify per item.
  • Creative Commons — free to use under conditions: BY requires credit, SA requires share-alike, NC excludes commercial use, ND forbids derivatives. Re-cutting an ND clip is a violation even with credit.
  • Stock and broadcast licences — permission limited by territory, term, media and audience size. A licence for a website does not cover a paid ad.
  • "Free to use" without a licence — a claim, not a grant. If there is no named licensor and no terms, assume nothing.

The line: backup versus republishing

Personal copies and public distribution are different acts with different consequences. The practical test is simple: does the file leave your devices, or does it reach an audience?

What you are doingWhat it usually needs
Archiving your own uploadsNothing — it is your work
Keeping a copy of a video you can already watchAwareness of local private-copy rules
Quoting a clip in commentary or reviewA genuine fair-use / quotation argument, kept short
Re-uploading someone's video to another platformPermission, or a licence that allows it
Using a clip in an ad or a paid courseA commercial licence, almost always
Building a channel from other people's uploadsDo not

If a rights holder objects

Notice-and-takedown systems exist because platforms are not courts. A notice identifies the work, the infringing location and a contact, and the host is expected to act promptly; a counter-notice is how you respond if you believe the notice is wrong. Responding to a notice is not an admission, and ignoring one is not a defence. The procedure that applies to this site is in the terms.

Not legal advice This note explains how the questions are usually framed. Copyright exceptions are national, fact-specific and change. For anything commercial, get advice from a lawyer in your jurisdiction.

The one-sentence version

Downloading is a technical act; whether it is permissible depends on what you take, why, and whether the copy ever leaves your own devices — and a platform's silence on the subject is not a licence from the person who made the work.
